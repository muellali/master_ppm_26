\section{Research Method}\label{section::method}

\subsection{Research Approach}\label{subsection::approach}

The planned thesis will follow the methodology of algorithm engineering by Mendling et al.~\cite{10.1145/3769071}. It will not develop a new algorithm. It will produce empirical knowledge about existing designs: bias-mitigation methods that have already been published will be tested in a setting where they have not been tested yet, a real emergency department process with real attributes of patients.

The study will have two parts. A descriptive part will come first. For each insurance group and each decision point, it will report how many visits there are, how often they end in an admission, how many visits reach the second decision point, and how the groups differ in acuity, age and mode of arrival. These numbers will help to interpret the later results. If fairness differs between the two decision points, the descriptive part will show whether different patients reach them. It will also show whether the groups are large enough, since fairness measures become unstable for small groups~\cite{sanchezmarques2026addressing}.

The second part will be an experiment with two factors: the mitigation method and the decision point at which the prediction is made.

\subsection{Data and Patient Groups}\label{subsection::data}

The data will come from MIMICEL, an event log built from MIMIC-IV-ED~\cite{wei2023mimicel}. Access to the data has not been granted yet. All numbers in this section are therefore taken from published studies and will be recomputed once the data is available. MIMICEL contains 425,028 stays of 205,466 patients in the emergency department of the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston~\cite{wei2025curation,lett2025intersectional}. Each stay is one case. The log has six activities: entering the ED, triage, vital sign check, medicine reconciliation, medicine dispensation and discharge from the ED~\cite{wei2025curation}.

Insurance is recorded only in the hospital admissions table of MIMIC-IV, so it is known only for visits that end in an admission~\cite{mimicdocs}. Taken from the visit itself, it would therefore reveal the outcome. The planned thesis will instead take insurance from the patient's most recent earlier hospital stay and will study only visits of patients with such a stay. This has two drawbacks: insurance may have changed since the earlier stay, and whether a patient has an earlier stay depends on past admission decisions. Table~\ref{table::insurance_groups} shows the insurance categories.

\subsection{Prediction Task and Model}\label{subsection::task}

The model will predict whether a visit ends in a hospital admission. A visit will count as admitted if its disposition is ``admitted''; all other dispositions will count as not admitted, including going home, transfer to another hospital and leaving without being seen. Licoppe et al.\ also derive a binary outcome from the disposition on MIMIC-IV-ED~\cite{licoppe2026assessing}. Two details will be checked once the data is available: 15\,\% of visits with the disposition ``home'' still have a hospital admission identifier~\cite{wei2025curation}, and coding transfers differently may change the results.

Predictions will be made at two decision points: at triage, and at the first medicine dispensation. At each point, the model will use only events and attributes recorded up to that moment, as is usual in outcome-oriented predictive process monitoring~\cite{teinemaa2019outcome}. The diagnosis, for example, is recorded only at the end of the visit~\cite{wei2025curation} and will therefore not be used.

Not every visit reaches the second decision point. Medicine is dispensed in 295,998 of the 425,028 stays, in 81.5\,\% of the most urgent stays but only 27.6\,\% of the least urgent ones~\cite{wei2025curation}. A difference between the two decision points could therefore come from the different patients rather than from the moment of prediction. To separate the two, the triage model will also be evaluated on only those visits that later reach the second decision point (Table~\ref{table::evaluation_plan}).

One limitation remains. The event log records when a patient leaves the ED, but not when the decision to admit was made. For some visits, the first medication may come after that decision, for example while the patient waits for a bed. A check after data access will show how often this happens.

The planned thesis will use XGBoost, a model based on gradient-boosted decision trees~\cite{chen2016xgboost}. It performed well in a benchmark of outcome-oriented predictive process monitoring~\cite{teinemaa2019outcome}. Gradient-boosted trees, including XGBoost, have been used to predict hospital admission from ED data~\cite{xie2022benchmarking,lett2025intersectional,king2022machine}. An LSTM is planned as an optional extension.

The data will be cleaned before training. Which steps are needed, for example for implausible or missing values, can only be decided once the data is available.

The visits will be split by time: the model will be trained on the oldest visits, tuned on a validation set of the following visits and tested on the most recent ones. This follows how a model is used in practice, where only past cases are known~\cite{teinemaa2019outcome}.

The performance measures will be chosen once the share of admitted visits in the data is known. If admissions are rare, measures that remain informative for rare outcomes will be used.

\subsection{Fairness Measures}\label{subsection::fairness_measures}

Fairness can be measured in many ways, and which measure fits depends on how the prediction is used~\cite{rajkomar2018ensuring}. Table~\ref{table::fairness_measures} lists the group measures described by Pohl et al.~\cite{pohl2023discrimination} and those used in related clinical studies, together with an assessment for the planned use. Individual and counterfactual fairness are not included, because they need an agreed measure of similarity between patients or a causal model of how insurance affects the other data~\cite{pohl2023discrimination,desantis2027fairness}.

The planned thesis will use equal opportunity and demographic parity as main measures. A predicted admission lets the hospital request a bed early~\cite{lett2025intersectional}, so the harmful error is a missed admission; Pohl et al.\ call this an assistive context~\cite{pohl2023discrimination}. Equal opportunity~\cite{hardt2016equality} compares exactly this error between the groups. It takes the recorded admissions as correct, however, although they are decisions by ED staff that can carry existing disparities~\cite{rajkomar2018ensuring} and have been found to differ by insurance~\cite{venkatesh2019association}. Demographic parity does not use them. Four additional measures cover what the main ones leave open. Equalized odds also counts unneeded bed requests and is widely used in related studies~\cite{liu2024faim,dasilva2025towards}, so the results can be compared with them. Predictive parity shows whether a predicted admission is equally reliable in both groups, which matters if staff decide whether to act on it~\cite{rajkomar2018ensuring}. The AUC gap~\cite{roosli2022peeking} and calibration within groups~\cite{pfohl2021empirical} need no threshold and can therefore be interpreted although the hospital's threshold is unknown. Demographic parity and predictive parity change with the admission rate. For these two, the evaluation on the same visits (Table~\ref{table::evaluation_plan}) separates the effect of the decision point from that of the different patients. The chosen measures cannot all be met at once when admission rates differ~\cite{paulus2020predictably}. Reporting them together shows what an intervention trades off and prevents it from being judged only by the measure it was built to optimise.

\subsection{Known Limitations}\label{subsection::limitations}

Several limitations of the planned thesis are already known.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Insurance from an earlier stay.} Insurance is recorded only for visits that end in an admission~\cite{mimicdocs} and will therefore be taken from the patient's most recent earlier stay. It may have changed since then, and only patients with an earlier stay can be studied. Whether a patient has an earlier stay depends on past admission decisions.
  \item \textbf{Results limited to two groups.} Medicare, the largest insured group, covers mainly older patients~\cite{roosli2022peeking} and will only be described, so the main results apply to Medicaid and privately insured patients.
  \item \textbf{Small groups.} After the restriction to patients with an earlier stay, the Medicaid group may be small, and fairness measures become unstable for small groups~\cite{sanchezmarques2026addressing}.
  \item \textbf{Recorded admissions as ground truth.} Admissions are decisions by ED staff that can carry existing disparities~\cite{rajkomar2018ensuring} and have been found to differ by insurance~\cite{venkatesh2019association}. Measures that compare predictions with recorded admissions, including equal opportunity, treat them as correct.
  \item \textbf{Timing of the admission decision.} The event log records when a patient leaves the ED, not when the decision to admit was made. For some visits, the first medication may come after this decision.
  \item \textbf{Different visits at the two decision points.} Not every visit reaches the first medication~\cite{wei2025curation}. The evaluation on the same visits controls this for the triage model, but the model for the second decision point can only be trained on visits that reach it.
  \item \textbf{Insurance at prediction time.} Group-specific thresholds need the insurance of every new patient and treat patients differently by insurance. They cannot be applied to patients without an earlier stay.
  \item \textbf{Differences, not causes.} The planned thesis will measure differences between insurance groups but will not estimate whether insurance causes them.
  \item \textbf{One hospital.} The data come from a single emergency department in Boston~\cite{wei2025curation,lett2025intersectional} and from the US insurance system, so the results may not carry over to other hospitals or countries.
  \item \textbf{No data access yet.} The numbers in this proposal are taken from published studies. Group sizes and admission rates may differ once they are recomputed.
\end{enumerate}
